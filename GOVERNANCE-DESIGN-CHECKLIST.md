# Governance Design Pre-Launch Checklist

**15 questions every GRC team should answer before standing up a program.**

Most GRC programs fail not because of bad frameworks or bad people, but because 
of bad design. This checklist is a diagnostic tool. Use it before you build, 
or use it to audit what you have already built. Either way, the answers will 
tell you where your program is structurally weak.

---

## How to use this checklist

Work through each question with your team. For every "no" or "we are not sure," 
treat it as a design gap, not a compliance gap. Document your answers. The 
conversation this checklist generates is as valuable as the answers themselves.

---

## Section 1: Ownership and Accountability

**1. Does every control in your program have a single named owner?**
Not a team. Not a department. A person whose name is on it and who knows 
they are responsible.

- [ ] Yes, every control has a named owner
- [ ] Some controls have named owners
- [ ] No, ownership is shared or undefined

**2. Does each control owner understand what "operating this control" looks 
like on a normal Tuesday?**
Ownership without operational clarity is decorative. The owner should be able 
to describe their control activities without referencing the policy document.

- [ ] Yes, owners can describe their day-to-day control activities
- [ ] Partially — some owners are clear, others are not
- [ ] No, ownership exists on paper only

**3. Is there a documented succession plan for control ownership?**
When a control owner leaves, does the control leave with them? If yes, 
that is a design flaw.

- [ ] Yes, succession is documented for all critical controls
- [ ] Partially documented
- [ ] No succession plan exists

---

## Section 2: Evidence and Audit Readiness

**4. Is evidence collection a byproduct of normal operations, or a 
separate activity?**
If your team has to "go collect evidence" as a distinct task, your 
governance processes are not integrated into your operations.

- [ ] Evidence is generated automatically through normal workflows
- [ ] Evidence is partially automated, partially manual
- [ ] Evidence collection is a separate manual activity

**5. Could you produce audit evidence for your top 10 controls within 
24 hours, without scrambling?**
This is the real test of audit readiness. Not whether you have the evidence 
eventually, but whether you have it now.

- [ ] Yes, evidence is continuously available
- [ ] We could produce it within a week
- [ ] No, it would require significant effort

**6. Do your evidence sources survive personnel changes?**
If the evidence lives in one person's inbox or a shared drive only they 
manage, it is not a sustainable evidence architecture.

- [ ] Yes, evidence is stored in centralized, access-controlled repositories
- [ ] Partially centralized
- [ ] Evidence is siloed by individual or team

**7. Is your evidence retention policy documented and enforced?**
Knowing what to keep and for how long is a design decision, not an 
afterthought.

- [ ] Yes, retention policy is documented and actively enforced
- [ ] Policy exists but enforcement is inconsistent
- [ ] No retention policy exists

---

## Section 3: Policy Operationalization

**8. Do your policies tell people what to do, or just what the rule is?**
A policy that says "access must be reviewed quarterly" is a rule. A policy 
that says "the system owner runs the access review report in [system] on 
the first Monday of each quarter and submits results to [role] by Friday" 
is an operationalized policy.

- [ ] Policies include specific procedural guidance
- [ ] Some policies are operationalized, others are not
- [ ] Policies are rules-based with no procedural detail

**9. Is there a defined review cycle for every policy, with a named 
reviewer?**
Policies that are never reviewed drift away from operational reality. 
Every policy should have an owner and a review date.

- [ ] Yes, every policy has a named reviewer and review schedule
- [ ] Some policies have review schedules
- [ ] No formal review cycle exists

**10. When a policy changes, is there a defined communication and 
training process?**
A policy update that does not reach the people it affects is not 
an update. It is a document change.

- [ ] Yes, change communication and training process is defined
- [ ] Ad hoc communication happens but no formal process
- [ ] No defined communication process exists

---

## Section 4: Risk Management Integration

**11. Has your risk register been meaningfully updated in the last 60 days?**
A static risk register is not a risk management tool. It is a 
risk documentation artifact. There is a difference.

- [ ] Yes, updated within the last 30 days
- [ ] Updated within the last 60 days
- [ ] Has not been updated in over 60 days

**12. Are risk treatment plans actively tracked to completion?**
Identifying risk without tracking treatment is half a program. 
The back half is where accountability lives.

- [ ] Yes, all treatment plans have owners, timelines, and status tracking
- [ ] Some treatment plans are tracked
- [ ] Risk identification exists but treatment tracking does not

**13. Is there a defined escalation path for risks that exceed your 
risk tolerance threshold?**
Your team should never have to improvise when a risk crosses a threshold. 
The path to leadership should be documented before it is needed.

- [ ] Yes, escalation paths are documented and tested
- [ ] Informal escalation exists but is not documented
- [ ] No defined escalation process exists

---

## Section 5: Program Sustainability

**14. Could your GRC program continue to operate effectively if your 
top GRC person left tomorrow?**
If the answer is no, your program is a person, not a system. 
Systems outlast individuals.

- [ ] Yes, documentation and processes would sustain the program
- [ ] Partially — some areas would struggle
- [ ] No, the program depends heavily on specific individuals

**15. Do your governance metrics tell you something actionable, or 
just something reportable?**
The difference between a metric that says "85% of controls are compliant" 
and one that says "these 12 controls are at risk of failure by Q3 for 
these specific reasons" is the difference between a dashboard and 
a decision tool.

- [ ] Metrics are actionable and drive decisions
- [ ] Metrics exist but are primarily for reporting
- [ ] No meaningful governance metrics exist

## Scoring Your Results

Count your responses:

| Response pattern | What it means |
|---|---|
| Mostly first option | Your program is well-engineered. Focus on continuous improvement. |
| Mix of first and second | Your program has a foundation. Prioritize the gaps in Sections 1 and 2 first. |
| Mostly second and third options | Your program is assembled, not engineered. Start with ownership and evidence architecture before anything else. |
| Mostly third option | Stop adding controls. Redesign the foundation first. More controls on a broken design make the problem worse. |

---

## What to do with your gaps

For every question where you answered the second or third option:

1. Name it as a design gap, not a compliance finding
2. Assign a single owner to close it
3. Set a 30, 60, or 90-day target based on severity
4. Track it the same way you track any governance action item

The goal is not a perfect score on this checklist. The goal is a program 
that functions reliably when pressure comes — an audit, an incident, 
a leadership change, or a bad quarter.
